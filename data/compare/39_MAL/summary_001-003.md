# Malachi 1–3 (KJV 1–4) — comparison summary

- **Chapters:** 1–3 in Hebrew numbering (55 verses; Hebrew 3:19–24 = KJV 4:1–6)
- **Flagged verses:** 18 (19 rows)

- **By severity:** 1 (nuance) 17 · 2 (notable) 2 · 3 (major) 0

## Systematic differences

- **Divine names:** KJV "the LORD" / "the LORD of hosts" for YHWH / YHWH Tseva'ot; rendered here Yahweh / Yahweh of Armies (not flagged per verse).
- **Chapter division:** the KJV (after the LXX/Vulgate) makes 3:19–24 a separate chapter 4; the text is the same.
- **massa'** ("burden", 1:1) = "oracle"; **nora'** ("dreadful", 1:14; 3:23) = "feared, awe-inspiring".
- **Textual base:** the KJV follows the Masoretic Text throughout; no textual divergences found.

## Most important differences

1. **2:16** — "he hateth putting away: for one covereth violence with his garment" vs "the man who hates and divorces... covers his garment with violence": *sane'* is a third-person participle, not "I hate", and the KJV reverses "garment" and "violence".
2. **1:10** — KJV complains that no priest will shut the doors without pay; the Hebrew is a wish that someone would shut the Temple doors and end worthless sacrifices.
3. **2:15** — "did not he make one? Yet had he the residue of the spirit" — a famously obscure verse; both renderings are attempts.
4. **3:17** — "when I make up my jewels" vs "my treasured possession, on the day that I act": *segullah* describes the God-fearers themselves.
5. **1:11** — KJV future "shall be great... shall be offered" for Hebrew verbless/participial clauses most naturally present.
6. **3:24 (KJV 4:6)** — "smite the earth with a curse" vs "strike the land with a ban of destruction" (*cherem*).
7. **1:5** — "magnified from the border of Israel" vs "great... beyond the borders of Israel".
8. **2:12** — "the master and the scholar" for the idiom *'er we'oneh* ("anyone at all").

## Assessment

The KJV renders Malachi's disputation speeches faithfully in structure and in most of their content, including the key passages on the coming messenger (3:1–4) and Elijah (3:23–24). Its weaknesses are concentrated in the difficult marriage passage (2:10–16), where it reverses one phrase and turns the grammar of "hate divorce" into a direct divine statement, and in a few idioms (1:10; 2:12) and rare words (*tannot* "jackals", *gazul* "stolen", *pishtem* "leap") that it misread. None of these changes the book's message substantially, but 1:10 and 2:16 would give a careful reader a noticeably different picture.
