# Exodus 1–40: summary

**Coverage:** 40 chapters, 1,213 verses.

**Flags:** 126 flags.
- By category: LEXICAL 34, ARCHAIC 32, MEANING 31, INTERP 26, NAME 2, TEXT 1.
- By severity: 99 at sev 1, 27 at sev 2.

## Systematic

- **"Borrow" for *sha'al* (3:22, 11:2, 12:35–36).** The Hebrew means "ask". The KJV has Israel "borrow" jewellery from the Egyptians. That implies a promise to give it back, which the text calls plunder, not a loan. The rendering has caused needless moral objections to the story.
- **"The judges" for *ha-elohim* (21:6, 22:7–9, 22:27).** The Hebrew normally means "God". In these case laws the parties come "to God", that is, to the sanctuary. The KJV follows the Targum and makes God into human judges.
- **"Meat offering" for *minchah*.** This is a grain offering. In 1611 "meat" meant food in general, but today it means flesh.
- **"Repent of the evil" (32:12, 14).** The Hebrew means "relent from the disaster".

## Most important differences

1. **20:13.** "Thou shalt not kill". *Ratsach* means murder. The same law code commands capital punishment and war.
2. **6:3.** "JEHOVAH". This hybrid form combines the consonants YHWH with the vowels of *Adonai*.
3. **7:13 and the hardening of Pharaoh's heart.** In several verses the Hebrew says Pharaoh's heart "grew hard" (intransitive). The KJV regularly makes God the active subject, which sharpens the theological problem.
4. **9:15–16.** The KJV has "I will stretch out my hand" and "have I raised thee up". The Hebrew is a counterfactual ("I could have…") followed by "I have let you remain". Paul's citation in Romans 9:17 adds to the theological weight of these verses.
5. **16:15.** "It is manna". The Hebrew *man hu'* is a question, "What is it?". The name of the food is a pun on that question.
6. **21:22.** The law on a miscarriage is ambiguous in both versions. It is noted for its use in modern debate.
7. **32:25.** "the people were naked". The Hebrew means they had "broken loose".

## Assessment

The KJV renders the exodus and Sinai narratives with great force and fidelity. The flags concentrate in three areas:
- a handful of systematic choices ("borrow", "judges", "meat", "repent");
- tabernacle vocabulary that was uncertain in 1611 ("badgers' skins", "groves");
- the hardening of Pharaoh's heart, where verb voice matters.

Several of these, especially "borrow" and "Thou shalt not kill", have had a large effect on how readers understand the moral meaning of the book.
