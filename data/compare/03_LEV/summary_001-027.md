# Leviticus 1–27: summary

**Coverage:** 27 chapters, 859 verses.

**Flags:** 91 flags.
- By category: LEXICAL 38, MEANING 18, INTERP 19, ARCHAIC 14, TEXT 2.
- By severity: 74 at sev 1, 17 at sev 2.

## Systematic

- **"Leprosy" for *tsara'at* (chapters 13–14).** The Hebrew term covers several skin conditions, and also mould on fabric and on the walls of houses (13:47–59, 14:34). It is not Hansen's disease. The KJV's "leprosy in a house" shows that the word cannot mean the disease.
- **"Of his own voluntary will" for *lirtsono* (1:3, 19:5, 22:19).** The phrase means "for his acceptance", that is, so that the offering is accepted by God. The KJV turns a statement about God accepting the offering into one about the worshipper's motive.
- **"Meat offering" for *minchah*.** This is the grain offering, as in Exodus. In 1611 "meat" meant food in general.

## Most important differences

1. **16:8, 10.** "the scapegoat" for *la'aza'zel*. The phrase is parallel to "for Yahweh", so it reads most naturally as a name: "for Azazel". The KJV coined "scapegoat" as a description and lost the name.
2. **17:7.** "devils" for *se'irim*, goat-demons of the wilderness.
3. **17:11.** "the blood maketh an atonement for the soul". The Hebrew more likely means "by means of the life" that is in the blood. The sentence is central to the theology of sacrifice.
4. **19:20.** "she shall be scourged". The word means an inquiry, or compensation.
5. **25:35.** "though he be a stranger". The poor *brother* is to be supported *as if* he were a resident alien. He is not himself a stranger.
6. **4:3.** The KJV obscures the point that the high priest's sin brings guilt on the whole people.

## Assessment

The KJV's Leviticus is careful but often opaque. Much of its ritual and medical vocabulary was uncertain in 1611, and the translators fell back on traditional guesses. The most widely felt effect is "leprosy": for centuries it attached this book's purity laws to a single dreaded disease. The coinage "scapegoat", by contrast, entered the English language in a sense the Hebrew does not have.
