The KJV against its sources

All 66 books were translated afresh from the original languages and compared with the King James Version of 1611, verse by verse. Every real difference in meaning was logged, sorted by cause, and graded for how much it changes what a reader would understand.

What was done

Sources. The Old Testament was translated from the Westminster Leningrad Codex (the Masoretic Text) and the New Testament from the SBL Greek New Testament. The KJV and the Greek text behind its New Testament (Scrivener's Textus Receptus) were set beside each translated chapter.

Flags. Each difference got one of six categories: TEXT (the underlying manuscript differs), MEANING, INTERP, LEXICAL, ARCHAIC or NAME. It also got a severity: 1 for nuance, 2 for a notable change in sense, 3 for a change that alters what the passage says.

Not flagged. Consistent conventions are recorded once per book, not at every verse. These include "the LORD" for YHWH, "Holy Ghost", and "hell" for Sheol and Hades.

Two testaments, two kinds of gap

The two testaments show almost the same rate of difference, about 7 flags per 100 verses each, but for opposite reasons. In the Old Testament the KJV and this translation read the same Hebrew text, and the differences are in how rare words, idioms and poetry were understood in 1611. In the New Testament the KJV translates a different Greek text, so most differences come from the manuscripts.

Flags by cause

Flags by severity

85% of flags are nuance. One in seven changes the sense noticeably. Thirteen alter what a passage says.

The thirteen major differences

These are the places where a KJV reader meets a meaningfully different text or claim. Five come from the Greek text behind the KJV. The other eight are places where the Hebrew can be read more than one way, and several are still debated: each shows how confident this translation is in its reading and, where the evidence is balanced, says that the KJV's reading is equally valid. A November 2026 review of the contested passages changed two former major differences, Job 13:15 and Psalm 22:16, which now read as the KJV does.

Ambiguous readings, decided by context

In 65 high-impact places the source can be read more than one way. For each of these, the surrounding verses were read and the likeliest meaning chosen. The context points clearly one way in 20 of them and leans one way in 38. In 7 it does not decide the question. Two translations changed as a result.

Changed by the context

  • Exodus 17:16. Now "a hand upon the banner of Yah", not "throne". The verse before names the altar "Yahweh is my banner", and Moses' raised hands were the sign of the battle.
  • 1 Chronicles 21:1. Now "Satan", not "an adversary". Without the article the Hebrew noun behaves as a name. The figure also incites the king where 2 Samuel has God doing it, and no human enemy is in the story. This now agrees with the KJV.

Where the context is decisive

  • Isaiah 7:14. The next two verses set the sign within Ahaz's own lifetime.
  • Psalm 22:16. The psalm pairs its animals in a symmetrical pattern: bulls, lion, dogs, then dog, lion, wild oxen. "Like a lion" completes that pattern.
  • Job 42:6. God then says Job spoke rightly, so it is not a confession of guilt.
  • Daniel 9:25. The next verse puts a second anointed one after the 62 weeks.
  • Revelation 22:14, 19. The book's own images are washed robes (7:14) and the tree of life (22:2).

Recurring patterns

Old Testament

  • Guesses at rare words. The KJV renders the wild ox as a "unicorn" (Numbers, Deuteronomy, Job, Psalms, Isaiah). It also has "badgers' skins", "mules" for hot springs, and "dragons" for jackals.
  • Cult vocabulary. The goddess Asherah's cult poles become "groves" throughout Kings and Chronicles. Shrine personnel become "sodomites". Goat-demons become "devils".
  • Messianic readings of ambiguous verses. Examples are Gen 49:10, Ps 22:16, Isa 7:14 and Dan 9:25, plus Job's three famous lines of faith.
  • Harmonising. At 2 Sam 21:19 the KJV adds "the brother of" so that Elhanan does not kill Goliath. In 2 Sam 8:18 and 20:26 David's sons are called "chief rulers" where the Hebrew says priests.
  • English that has shifted. Older senses of "replenish", "tempt", "prevent", "let", "meat offering" and "Thou shalt not kill" now mislead.

New Testament

  • Additions in the Greek text behind the KJV. 61% of NT flags are textual. Fifteen whole verses in the KJV have no counterpart in the critical text, among them Mt 17:21, Mk 9:44 and Acts 8:37.
  • Three long passages. The ending of Mark (16:9–20), the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11), and the Johannine Comma (1 John 5:7–8).
  • Readings found in no Greek manuscript. Acts 9:5–6 and Rev 22:16–21 come from Erasmus translating the Latin back into Greek. "And shalt be" at Rev 16:5 is Beza's conjecture.
  • Not always the higher Christology. In several places the critical text says more about Christ than the KJV does. At John 1:18 it calls him "the only God", and at Jude 5 it names Jesus as the one who led Israel out of Egypt. At Titus 2:13 and 2 Pet 1:1 the Greek names him "our God and Saviour", which the KJV's wording obscures.
  • Translation slips. "Easter" at Acts 12:4, "Jesus" for Joshua at Acts 7:45 and Heb 4:8, "faith" for hope at Heb 10:23, and "the root of all evil" at 1 Tim 6:10.

Book by book

Malachi has the highest rate (35 per 100 verses) and Haggai the lowest (3). Short books swing widely, so the rates are most meaningful for the longer books. Revelation has the highest share of textual flags: 71%, because Erasmus had only one incomplete manuscript of it.

What this means for a KJV reader

The KJV is a careful and, in most places, accurate translation of the texts its translators had. On 93% of verses it says what the source says. Most of the remaining differences are small.

The real gaps are of two kinds. In the Old Testament, a handful of contested verses were settled in the direction of Christian doctrine, and a number of rare words were guessed wrongly. In the New Testament, the Greek text it translated carries later additions. Among them are two long passages, the ending of Mark and the woman taken in adultery, and one doctrinal formula, the Johannine Comma, that the earliest manuscripts do not contain.

Limits. The Textus Receptus was checked at known variant sites and selected verses, not compared word by word across every verse. The SBLGNT apparatus was scanned for every place where the SBLGNT leaves out words that NA28 prints. Romans 16:25–27 is the only passage of whole verses, and it has been added. Five shorter phrases were also found and checked. The severity grades are one translator's judgement. The flag notes give the manuscript evidence or Hebrew grammar behind each grade, so each can be checked.

Data. Every flag, with the KJV wording, the new rendering and a note, is in results/flags.tsv. Totals are in results/stats.json. The 65 context verdicts are in results/ambiguities.tsv, each with its reasoning and a confidence level. Each book has a short summary in data/compare/<book>/.
